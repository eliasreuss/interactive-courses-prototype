Bullwhip Effect — Danish: Bullwhip-effekten
The bullwhip effect is the phenomenon where small swings in actual end demand grow larger and larger the further up the supply chain you go. A minor change at the end customer becomes a bigger change at the retailer, a larger one at the wholesaler, and a very large one at the manufacturer and raw material supplier.
Why does it happen?
Each tier orders not against real demand but against the order pattern it sees from the tier below, adding a buffer on top. Four things typically drive it:
Every tier forecasts on its own instead of sharing actual sales data
Orders are batched into larger lots to earn volume discounts or save freight
Promotions and price swings make customers buy ahead
When supply is unreliable, customers over-order to protect themselves
What does it cost?
The bullwhip effect produces alternating excess and shortage. The result is high tied-up capital, obsolete inventory and expedited purchases — and backorders when the swing reverses. It bites hardest far up the chain, where lead times are longest.
How do you dampen it?
Share actual sales figures upstream rather than only orders
Order more frequently in smaller quantities instead of rare large batches
Smooth promotions and price changes so demand is not artificially volatile
Use stable planning parameters rather than manual judgement reacting to last month
The shorter the distance between real demand and the decision being made, the smaller the whip.
