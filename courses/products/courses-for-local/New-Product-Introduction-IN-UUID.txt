index_title: Are We Introducing the Right Products?
title: Are We Introducing the Right Products?
type: main
layout: standard-2-col
---
New products are not always introduced based on data.

A customer asks for something. A supplier presents a new product. Or a new variant simply looks like a good opportunity, even though we may already have several similar products.

The result can be more products on the shelf without enough demand behind them. This is adding unhealthy complexity.
![video/size:large/align:right/frame:none](https://pub-3c39fac4c588469d9bf40bf9e56bdbaf.r2.dev/Products/Overfilled-Shelf.mp4)

slide_id: 5c2aead5-f16b-48ce-8d1e-1439354cbadf
index_title: What Happens After Launch?
title: What Happens After Launch?
type: main
layout: standard-2-col
---
The problem is not just that new products add complexity. Many of them never develop into strong products.

Exact failure rates vary by industry and by how failure is measured, but estimates often place new product failure at around **30 to 50 percent,** with some industries reporting even higher rates.

That makes early follow up critical. The sooner we see that a new product is falling behind, the sooner we can decide what to do about it.

**If we do not follow up on new products, today’s new introductions can become tomorrow’s dead stock.**
![video/size:large/align:right/frame:none](https://pub-3c39fac4c588469d9bf40bf9e56bdbaf.r2.dev/Products/Failing-Products.mp4)

title: What Do We Do With Products Already Introduced?
type: main
layout: standard-2-col
---
The framework becomes useful when we decide what good performance should look like.

For example, we could set a target that 80% of new products should develop towards the healthy side of the portfolio.

If a product falls behind, we can act while there is still time.

* Can we adjust the price?
* Should we contact the customer?
* Does the product need more commercial attention?

**The goal is to deal with weak introductions early, instead of discovering them as dead stock two years later.**
![image/size:large/align:right/frame:none](products-new-product-introduction/Already-Introduced.png)

index_title: Which Products Should We Introduce in the Future?
title: Which Products Should We Introduce in the Future?
type: main
layout: standard-2-col
---
Following new products also changes how we make future decisions.

If you know a new product will be reviewed after a few months, it becomes natural to ask harder questions before introducing it:

* Do we already have a similar product?
* Is there a real customer behind the request?
* Have we spoken to the customer first?

Sometimes the best decision is not to introduce the product at all.
![image/size:large/align:right/frame:none](products-new-product-introduction/Introduce-In-Future.png)

index_title: What Should We Discuss With Our Suppliers?
title: What Should We Discuss With Our Suppliers?
type: main
layout: standard-2-col
---
Poor performance does not always mean the product itself is bad.

A supplier may tell you that the same product is performing well with other customers or in other markets.

If that is the case, the question changes:

* **Why is it not working for us?** Is it the price? The positioning? The customer base? Or the way we introduced it?

The data gives you a better starting point for that conversation.
![image/size:550px/align:right/frame:none](products-new-product-introduction/Discuss-With-Suppliers.png)
